%% Abstract                          %%
%%==================================%%

\abstract{Cloud-native systems are observable but non-stationary: the telemetry that machine-learning detectors consume is continuously reshaped by deployments, autoscaling and shifting traffic, so a decision boundary that is accurate at training time silently decays in production. Existing studies of observability-driven anomaly detection almost universally evaluate a model that is trained once on a static snapshot, and therefore measure a \emph{stationary ceiling} that may not survive contact with a drifting stream. This paper asks a different question: when the stream drifts, does the \emph{learning paradigm}---static-batch, periodically-retrained, or continuously-online---matter more than the richness of the telemetry, and how do the two interact? We construct a production-representative testbed, TraceFlix, a Kubernetes microservice application instrumented end-to-end with OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, Loki and Tempo, and drive it through four ordered telemetry configurations (metrics-only through full Metrics--Events--Logs--Traces) and four operating regimes that inject realistic drift (latency regression, scale-out, and combined load). On a held-out baseline set, detection quality rises monotonically with telemetry richness (F1 $0.896 \rightarrow 0.986$), with distributed traces the single most valuable signal and ensemble trees the strongest model family; traces also lift root-cause top-1 localisation from $0.77$ to a perfect $1.0$ on a deep nine-service mesh. But under drift the picture inverts: a frozen batch model collapses to F1 $\approx 0.36$ \emph{regardless} of how much telemetry it is given, scheduled retraining recovers most of the gap (up to $\sim 0.93$), and a lightweight online-adaptive detector dominates once the telemetry is rich enough---decisively at C3/C4 and in two-thirds of operating regimes---reaching F1 $\approx 0.98$ under full telemetry and even exceeding an all-regimes batch oracle, while under thin telemetry it runs within $0.007$ F1 of periodic retraining. The online detector is additionally $9$--$20\times$ better on tail latency, $\sim 130$--$390\times$ smaller, and retains no historical data. We conclude that telemetry completeness and continual learning are complementary rather than substitutable: richer signals raise the attainable ceiling, but only adaptation realises it on a live, drifting system.}

\keywords{Observability, Anomaly Detection, Concept Drift, Online Learning, Cloud-Native Systems, Machine Learning, Microservices}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%%===========================================================%%

\subsection{The Problem and Its Significance}\label{sec:intro-problem}\label{sec:motivation}

Cloud-native systems---collections of loosely coupled microservices deployed on container orchestrators such as Kubernetes---have become the dominant architecture for large-scale online services \cite{cncf2024survey}. Their elasticity and rate of change are precisely what make them difficult to operate. A modern deployment may roll out new code many times per day \cite{dora2024}, autoscale pods up and down in response to traffic, reschedule workloads across heterogeneous nodes, and degrade partially in ways that never fully crash any single component. The very properties that make these systems resilient also make their failures diffuse, intermittent and hard to attribute \cite{mohammad2025resilient}. Traditional threshold-based monitoring, in which an operator hand-codes a static alarm for each metric, scales neither to the number of signals nor to the rate at which their normal ranges shift \cite{islam2021,mart2020}.

The response of the field has been \emph{observability}: the practice of instrumenting a system so richly that its internal state can be reconstructed from the telemetry it emits \cite{kosinska2023,boten2022}. The telemetry is conventionally organised into the MELT model---Metrics, Events, Logs and Traces---and a mature observability stack federates all four through vendor-neutral instrumentation such as OpenTelemetry and storage backends such as Prometheus, Loki and Tempo \cite{boten2022,chapman2023}. Observability turns the operational problem into a data problem, and a data problem invites machine learning. A substantial literature now applies classical, deep and graph-based models to observability data, both to \emph{detect} that something has gone wrong and to \emph{localise} the responsible service \cite{lomio2022,dkmak2025,han2024,wang2018,chen2025,raeiszadeh2026}.

Two assumptions run, mostly unexamined, through this literature. The first is that \emph{more telemetry is better}: that adding logs, then traces, then events monotonically improves detection. The second, more consequential, assumption is that the world is \emph{stationary}: that a model trained once on a representative snapshot will continue to perform once it is deployed. Almost every reported result is obtained by training a model on a fixed dataset and evaluating it on a held-out portion of the \emph{same} distribution \cite{lomio2022,dkmak2025,han2024}; the few datasets in common use are themselves narrow, and even on them headline numbers prove fragile under stricter evaluation \cite{landauer2022logsurvey,sarfraz2024quovadis}. This protocol measures what we will call the \emph{stationary ceiling}---the best a detector can do when nothing changes. It is silent on what happens afterwards, when a deployment shifts a service's latency profile, an autoscaler doubles its replica count, or a traffic surge moves every metric's baseline at once. In those moments the data-generating distribution drifts, and a decision boundary fitted to the old normal begins to mislabel the new normal as anomalous.

This paper takes that second assumption as its subject. We argue that for a non-stationary observability stream the dominant design variable is not how much telemetry a detector consumes but \emph{whether and how it keeps learning}. We make this concrete by studying two orthogonal axes together. The first axis is the familiar one of \emph{telemetry completeness}: four ordered configurations from metrics-only to full MELT. The second, which the existing literature rarely treats as a variable at all, is the \emph{learning paradigm}: a static-batch model trained once and frozen; a periodically-retrained model refitted on a sliding buffer; and an online-adaptive model that updates incrementally on every window. Crossing these axes lets us ask not only ``does observability matter?'' but ``\emph{when} does it matter, and compared to what?''

A concrete vignette fixes the problem. A team trains a detector on a representative week and ships it; it performs beautifully in validation. A fortnight later a caching change raises a service's median latency by a modest, entirely healthy margin while an autoscaler doubles its replicas during a marketing push. Nothing is wrong, yet every latency and per-replica-utilisation feature has shifted, and the frozen detector---its notion of ``normal'' fixed a fortnight ago---scores this healthy traffic as anomalous and pages the on-call engineer through the night. The remaining options are unappealing: silence the detector (losing coverage), hand-tune thresholds (the toil observability was meant to remove), or retrain (which lags the next such change by a full cycle). This is the ordinary weekly life of a cloud-native service, and the precise scenario RQ3 isolates and measures.

Three observations motivate the study. First, instrumentation is not free: active tracing imposes runtime overhead, and the volume, velocity and variety of telemetry impose real storage and processing costs \cite{kosinska2023,leadingobs,hausenblas2023roi,huang2025mint}, so a practitioner needs evidence on the \emph{marginal} detection value of each pillar, not a blanket assurance that more is better. Second, the field's most recent systematic surveys converge on the same gap from two directions: Raeiszadeh et al.\ \cite{raeiszadeh2026} find existing detectors ``systematically fail'' on adaptability to dynamic topologies, while Hrusto et al.\ \cite{hrusto2026} find most techniques are validated on data that does not represent the drift and heterogeneity of real operations---exactly the two things a static, single-snapshot evaluation cannot probe. Third, industrial experience reports the failure mode directly: at IBM Cloud scale, threshold and static models were found inadequate precisely because ``normal'' is a moving target \cite{islam2021}.

Whether that answer is ``more telemetry'' or ``keep the model current'' is not an academic distinction: it decides where a practitioner should spend a finite instrumentation budget and how a detector should be engineered. The governing question of this article is therefore:

\begin{quote}\itshape Does observability matter for real-time anomaly detection in cloud-native systems---and if so, is it the \emph{amount} of telemetry that decides detection quality, or \emph{whether the detector keeps learning} as the system changes?\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}\label{sec:rqs}

We approach this question with a controlled study that fixes the detection pipeline and varies, orthogonally, the telemetry it is given and the way it learns, on a single production-representative testbed under controlled fault injection and controlled drift. Because production is non-stationary, the operative measurements are taken on a drifting future stream; a controlled, single-distribution held-out slice---the stationary ceiling that single-snapshot studies report---serves only as a clean reference against which the field's prevailing intuitions can be checked. The most basic of those intuitions is that more telemetry is better, so the first question we ask is:

\rqbox{RQ1.}{Under non-stationary conditions, does the completeness of observability data---metrics-only, ${+}$logs, ${+}$traces, full MELT---improve the accuracy of anomaly detection, and which pillar contributes the largest marginal gain?}

\noindent RQ1 establishes whether, and through which pillar, richer telemetry raises detection once the stream is non-stationary. Detection is only half of operations, however: an alert becomes actionable only when the responsible service is named, and because traces alone carry the cross-service causal structure that metrics and logs lack, we isolate their contribution to localisation:

\rqbox{RQ2.}{Which observability pillar makes significant contribution for root-cause localisation, holding all other signals constant?}

\noindent Completeness and localisation describe what the telemetry can do; they leave open the question that the non-stationarity of production makes decisive---not \emph{how much} telemetry a detector consumes, but \emph{whether it keeps learning} as the system changes. A boundary fitted to yesterday's normal silently decays as deployments, autoscaling and shifting load reshape the telemetry. This is the crux of the article:

\rqbox{RQ3.}{When the telemetry stream is non-stationary, how do the static-batch, periodically-retrained and online-adaptive learning paradigms compare on detection quality \emph{and} operational cost, and how does this comparison interact with telemetry completeness?}

\noindent RQ3 shows which learning paradigm production demands; that settles the operational question but leaves a modelling one, which we therefore take up last. Having established the online setting, we ask which model family the pipeline should use and what that choice implies for the streaming detector:

\rqbox{RQ4.}{Which model family best exploits rich, multimodal observability features, and what does that choice imply for the model that must run in the online setting?}

\noindent RQ1 and RQ2 engage the established completeness and localisation intuitions of the observability literature, now under realistic non-stationarity, so that their results can be read against prior expectations; RQ3 makes the learning paradigm the explicit variable, and RQ4 reconciles the offline model-selection evidence with the online detector that RQ3 shows to be necessary. Throughout, the \emph{identical} pipeline and feature sets are re-used across the held-out reference and the drifting stream, so that any reversal of conclusions is attributable to drift and the learning paradigm rather than to a change of model, data, or task.

\subsection{Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

To answer these questions we built \emph{TraceFlix}, a Kubernetes microservice application instrumented end-to-end with OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, Loki and Tempo, and drove it through four ordered (nested) telemetry configurations and four operating regimes under controlled fault injection---a design that crosses \emph{signal richness} with the \emph{learning paradigm} while holding the model architecture, feature engineering, fault scenarios and random seed fixed. To our knowledge this is the first study to vary both axes together. The article makes the following contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A two-axis experimental methodology} for observability-driven detection that isolates telemetry completeness (four nested configurations) and the learning paradigm (static, periodic, online) under controlled drift on a production-representative testbed (\S\ref{sec:problem}, \S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Empirical findings that answer the research questions}, established over the full configuration $\times$ paradigm $\times$ regime grid:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item[\emph{To RQ1:}] under non-stationarity, telemetry completeness still improves detection: for a detector that keeps current, drifted-stream F1 rises \emph{monotonically} from $0.81$ to $0.98$ across the configurations (mirroring $0.896 \rightarrow 0.986$ on a held-out reference), with distributed traces the single most valuable pillar---and decisively so under drift, where the trace increment ($+0.146$ F1) dwarfs that of logs or events.
    \item[\emph{To RQ2:}] adding traces lifts root-cause top-1 localisation from $0.77$ to a perfect $1.0$ on a deep nine-service mesh (where multi-hop latency leaves top-$k$ unsaturated without traces), isolating tracing as the decisive localisation signal.
    \item[\emph{To RQ3:}] under drift the result \emph{inverts}---a frozen batch model collapses to F1 $\approx 0.36$ \emph{regardless} of telemetry, periodic retraining recovers much of the gap, and a lightweight online-adaptive detector dominates once telemetry is rich (C3/C4) and in $8$ of $12$ regime cells, reaching F1 $\approx 0.98$ and beating an all-regimes oracle at every configuration, while being $9$--$20\times$ better on tail latency and $\sim 130$--$390\times$ smaller; under thin telemetry it and periodic retraining are within $0.007$ F1.
    \item[\emph{To RQ4:}] ensemble trees are the strongest family on multimodal tabular telemetry, which is also \emph{why a lightweight normalised linear model---not a tree---is the right detector for the online setting} established by RQ3.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An operational-cost analysis} that reframes the choice of learning paradigm as a service-level rather than a purely accuracy decision (\S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{A streaming system realisation and a local-LLM detector.} The online paradigm is deployed as an Apache Kafka event backbone \cite{kreps2011kafka} over a nine-microservice mesh with real per-service business logic, and the detector family is extended with a local LLM (Qwen2.5-3B \cite{qwen2025} via Ollama \cite{ollama2024}, optionally LoRA-tuned \cite{hu2022lora}) that reasons over \emph{raw} signals on the same stream. We contribute its architecture and three accompanying flowcharts (\S\ref{sec:method-streaming}); its served-model evaluation is reported as ongoing, and we do not claim an accuracy figure for it.
  \item \textbf{Practical guidance} on minimum-viable observability and on when continual learning is mandatory rather than optional (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}

To support open science, the testbed configuration, pipeline code and result datasets underpinning every table are available for reproducibility.

\section{Background and a Taxonomy of Cloud-Native Anomaly Detection}\label{sec:foundations}
%%===========================================================%%

Before formalising the study it is worth fixing vocabulary and, more usefully, drawing a map of the field the study inhabits. Cloud-native anomaly detection is not a single technique but a family of them, and the choices that distinguish one detector from another---what telemetry it consumes, what model it fits, how it learns as the system changes, and what it is asked to decide---are precisely the choices this paper treats as variables rather than as fixed background assumptions. This section establishes the concepts the rest of the article relies on (\S\ref{sec:fnd-concepts}) and organises the field into a taxonomy of detection approaches (\S\ref{sec:fnd-taxonomy}, Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy}) and locates within it the region this study occupies. The detailed, study-by-study comparison against the most directly related work is deferred to \S\ref{sec:background}, once our own results are in hand.

\subsection{Concepts and vocabulary}\label{sec:fnd-concepts}

\paragraph{Observability and the MELT model.} A system is \emph{observable} when its internal state can be reconstructed from its external outputs \cite{kosinska2023}. In a microservice deployment those outputs are telemetry, conventionally organised into the MELT model: \emph{metrics} (numeric time series such as latency percentiles or CPU utilisation), \emph{events} (discrete lifecycle records such as a pod restart), \emph{logs} (timestamped, semi-structured text), and \emph{traces} (per-request trees of spans that record how a request propagates across services) \cite{boten2022,chapman2023}. The pillars differ both in operational cost and in the information they carry: metrics are cheap and aggregate, logs are semantically rich but unstructured, traces alone encode the cross-service causal structure of a request---and are the most expensive pillar to collect and store---and events mark coarse state transitions. That a richer pillar costs more is exactly what makes the marginal detection value of each one an empirical question rather than a foregone conclusion.

\paragraph{The detection task and its relatives.} A detector consumes a window of telemetry and decides whether the system is behaving normally. Three operational tasks are usefully separated. \emph{Detection} asks whether something is wrong; \emph{localisation}, or root-cause analysis, asks which service or signal is responsible \cite{han2024}; and \emph{prediction} asks whether a violation is imminent, so that remediation can precede impact \cite{mart2020}. The three are sequential in operations---one must detect before localising---and they make different demands on the telemetry. This article concerns detection and localisation.

\paragraph{Non-stationarity and concept drift.} A cloud-native telemetry stream is \emph{non-stationary}. \emph{Concept drift} denotes a change over time in the joint distribution $P(\mathbf{x}, y)$ of features $\mathbf{x}$ and labels $y$ \cite{arora2024drift,lukats2025drift}. In operational terms, a deployment alters the telemetry that healthy behaviour produces, an autoscaling event shifts resource-utilisation baselines, and a traffic surge moves latency and throughput distributions at once; a decision boundary fitted to last week's normal then misclassifies this week's. Whether, and how, a detector copes with drift is therefore not a peripheral implementation detail but a defining property---and it is one of the two axes this study manipulates.

The approaches differ chiefly in the function they fit. \emph{Statistical and threshold} detectors alarm when a signal leaves a hand-set or forecast band---cheap and interpretable but brittle as baselines move \cite{mart2020,islam2021}; \emph{classical machine learning} (tree ensembles, isolation forests, one-class methods) remains a strong baseline on tabular telemetry \cite{lomio2022}; \emph{deep learning} dominates the recent literature in reconstruction \cite{dkmak2025}, sequential \cite{wang2025mtssurvey} and graph \cite{chen2023tracegra,akmeemana2025galmad,han2024} sub-families; \emph{LLM-based} detectors read raw logs and heterogeneous telemetry directly \cite{guan2024logllm,zhuang2024multimodal}; and \emph{multimodal MELT-fusion} detectors combine all four pillars into a joint representation, the end at which the strongest recent results cluster \cite{han2024,wang2025multimodal}.

Two choices cut across every branch yet are easy to overlook. The first is the \emph{learning paradigm}---trained once and frozen (static), refitted on a sliding buffer (periodic), or updated incrementally (online) \cite{wette2024omlad,li2023autoencoder}---orthogonal to the technique, since any family can be deployed in any paradigm. The second is the \emph{evaluation setting}: a stationary single-snapshot slice versus a moving, drift-aware one \cite{lukats2025drift}, on a public benchmark or synthetic generator \cite{bakhtin2025lo2,pham2025rcaeval}, an operational system \cite{islam2021,hrusto2026}, or---the middle path this study takes---a production-representative testbed under controlled fault injection and drift. Read against this map the field is unevenly worked: the technique branches are crowded and fast-moving, but these two cross-cutting choices are nearly collapsed---the overwhelming majority of detectors are trained once and evaluated on a stationary slice of a single distribution \cite{lomio2022,dkmak2025,han2024}, even though adaptability to a moving distribution is a named and systematically unmet requirement \cite{raeiszadeh2026}. This paper therefore holds the detection technique fixed across its main comparisons and varies the two under-explored choices together---telemetry completeness and the learning paradigm under controlled drift---while asking, last, which technique family best exploits the richest telemetry.

%%===========================================================%%
\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}
%%===========================================================%%

We now make the task and the two design axes precise: the \emph{telemetry-completeness} axis and the \emph{learning-paradigm} axis identified in the taxonomy of Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy}, whose crossing was singled out there as the under-explored region this study occupies.

\subsection{Windowed detection}\label{sec:prob-detect}

Let the system emit a continuous multimodal telemetry stream. We discretise time into fixed-length, non-overlapping windows $t = 1, 2, \dots$. For each window a configuration-dependent feature-extraction function $\phi_c$ maps the raw telemetry in that window to a feature vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_t^{(c)} = \phi_c(\text{telemetry}_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c},
\end{equation}
where $c \in \{C1, C2, C3, C4\}$ indexes the telemetry configuration and $d_c$ is the configuration's feature dimensionality (Table~\ref{tab:configs}). Each window carries a ground-truth binary label $y_t \in \{0,1\}$ ($1 =$ anomalous), derived from the fault-injection schedule (\S\ref{sec:method-faults}). A detector is a function $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{d_c} \to \{0,1\}$ with parameters $\theta$; its prediction is $\hat{y}_t = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t^{(c)})$. Detection quality is scored by comparing $\{\hat{y}_t\}$ with $\{y_t\}$ using the metrics of \S\ref{sec:method-metrics}.

Holding $f$, its hyper-parameter search, its preprocessing and its random seed fixed while varying only $c$ isolates \emph{signal availability} as the independent variable; this is the mechanism behind RQ1--RQ2. The configurations are \emph{nested}, each a superset of the previous, so the marginal contribution of each added pillar is the incremental change between adjacent configurations.

Detection quality is quantified from the confusion counts over a window set: true positives $TP$ (anomalous windows correctly flagged), false positives $FP$ (normal windows flagged), true negatives $TN$ and false negatives $FN$ (missed anomalies). Precision, recall and their harmonic mean are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Prec} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \quad
  \mathrm{Rec} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \quad
  \mathrm{F1} = \frac{2\,\mathrm{Prec}\cdot\mathrm{Rec}}{\mathrm{Prec}+\mathrm{Rec}}.
\end{equation}
F1 is the headline metric because the window labels are heavily imbalanced toward the normal class; under imbalance, accuracy $(TP{+}TN)/(TP{+}TN{+}FP{+}FN)$ is dominated by $TN$ and a trivial always-normal predictor scores highly while detecting nothing. AUC-ROC complements F1 by summarising the precision--recall trade-off across all decision thresholds, characterising ranking quality independently of the operating point.

The decomposition into precision and recall is not merely descriptive; it is diagnostic of the failure mode we study. A detector whose decision boundary has become \emph{stale} after drift does not fail symmetrically. Because the post-drift ``normal'' has moved into the region the boundary assigns to the anomalous class, the detector keeps firing on healthy traffic---recall on true anomalies may even stay high, but precision collapses as $FP$ grows. The stale-normal failure is therefore visible as a \emph{precision} collapse with preserved recall, and F1, by penalising the imbalance between the two, captures it where accuracy would not (\S\ref{sec:res-rq3-regime}).

\subsection{Drift and the temporal axis}\label{sec:prob-drift}

Let $P_t(\mathbf{x}, y)$ denote the joint distribution generating window $t$. We say the stream exhibits \emph{concept drift} if $P_t \neq P_{t'}$ for some $t \neq t'$. The streaming-learning literature distinguishes two forms of drift \cite{arora2024drift}, both of which our regimes induce. \emph{Virtual drift} is a change in the feature distribution $P_t(\mathbf{x})$ with the conditional $P_t(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ unchanged---for example, a scale-out event shifts the resource-utilisation features of healthy pods without changing what ``anomalous'' means. \emph{Real} (concept) drift is a change in $P_t(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ itself---for example, a latency level that was anomalous under the baseline becomes the new normal after a deployment regresses the service. Real drift is the more damaging for a static detector, because it directly invalidates the learned decision boundary rather than merely the input scaling; our regimes deliberately combine both so that neither a pure re-normalisation nor a pure re-fitting strategy would suffice alone. Drift is further characterised by its temporal profile---\emph{abrupt} (a step change at a regime boundary) versus \emph{gradual}; our regime transitions are abrupt, which is the harder case for periodic retraining because the lag between drift onset and the next scheduled refit is maximally exposed.

We operationalise drift as a sequence of \emph{regimes} $R_0, R_1, \dots$, where within a regime the distribution is approximately stationary and between regimes an operational event (latency regression, scale-out, combined load; \S\ref{sec:method-regimes}) shifts it. Regime $R_0$ is the baseline on which the offline models may train; the future stream $R_1, R_2, R_3$ is drifted relative to $R_0$.

The temporal axis is the \emph{learning policy} $\pi$ that maps the observed prefix of the stream to the parameters used at each window:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Static} ($\pi_{\text{stat}}$): fit $\theta$ once on $R_0$; $\theta_t = \theta_0$ for all $t$.
  \item \textbf{Periodic} ($\pi_{\text{per}}$): refit $\theta$ every $\tau$ windows on the most recent $B$ labelled windows; $\theta_t$ is piecewise-constant, updated at $t = \tau, 2\tau, \dots$.
  \item \textbf{Online} ($\pi_{\text{on}}$): update $\theta$ on every window via \emph{prequential} (test-then-train) processing---predict $\hat{y}_t$ from $\theta_{t-1}$, then update to $\theta_t$ using $(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)$.
\end{itemize}
A reference \emph{oracle} ($\pi_{\text{full}}$) is fit once on data drawn from \emph{all} regimes; it is not realisable online (it sees the future) and serves only as an upper bound on what a single static boundary could achieve if drift were known in advance.

The online policy is evaluated \emph{prequentially} (predictive-sequential): at each window the current model first predicts and is scored, then is updated with the now-revealed label. This protocol, standard in stream learning, has two properties we rely on. It uses every window for both testing and training without a held-out split, which is appropriate when data arrive as an unbounded stream; and, because each prediction is made strictly before the corresponding label is incorporated, it never leaks future information into a current prediction, so the reported online F1 is an honest estimate of what the deployed detector would have achieved in real time. The offline policies are scored on the same future windows for comparability: $\pi_{\text{stat}}$ predicts all future windows from its $R_0$-fit boundary, and $\pi_{\text{per}}$ from whichever boundary was most recently refitted before each window.

\subsection{The two-axis design}\label{sec:prob-design}

The study crosses the two axes, as Figure~\ref{fig:design} depicts. The \emph{completeness} axis sweeps $c \in \{C1{-}C4\}$: RQ1 reads it for detection---on the drifted future stream for the deployable continual-learning detector, with a held-out slice as a stationary reference---and RQ2 reads it for localisation on that held-out slice (the C2-vs-C3 contrast), while RQ4 fixes $c=\text{C4}$ and varies the model family. \section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Research system design and philosophy}\label{sec:method-design}

The study adopts a positivist, deductive stance realised as a controlled, repeated-measures experiment, consistent with empirical software engineering. The relationships of interest are causal and comparative (``does \emph{X} affect detection?''), so the design isolates each independent variable while holding confounds constant. Two factors are manipulated---telemetry completeness $c$ and learning policy $\pi$---and the dependent variables are the detection-quality and operational-cost metrics of \S\ref{sec:method-metrics}. The control variables (model architecture, feature preprocessing, fault scenarios, load profile, random seed) are fixed across cells so that identical episodes are observed under differing configurations and policies. This within-subjects design is deliberate: because every cell sees the same underlying faults and the same drift schedule, differences cannot be ascribed to sampling variation between scenario sets.

\subsection{The TraceFlix testbed}\label{sec:method-testbed}

The platform is \emph{TraceFlix}, a Kubernetes microservice application representing a realistic cloud-native system. The mesh comprises nine instrumented services with a genuine multi-hop dependency structure: a \texttt{gateway-service} entrypoint fans out to a \texttt{movie-service} subtree (\texttt{movie} issues $N$ sequential calls to \texttt{actor-service} and a call to \texttt{review-service}) and to \texttt{user}, \texttt{search} and \texttt{recommendation} services that converge on a shared \texttt{catalog-service} leaf---a call graph of depth four with fan-out and a fan-in. A request therefore traverses a multi-hop caller/callee chain, and a fault deep in the graph induces secondary latency in \emph{every} ancestor on its path. This exercises cross-service signal propagation for the localisation task on a graph large enough that top-$k$ attribution is non-trivial (\S\ref{sec:res-rq2}), yet controlled enough that the ground-truth origin is never in doubt.

Instrumentation is end-to-end and vendor-neutral, matching what a production OpenTelemetry deployment emits, which maximises the external validity of the telemetry schema \cite{boten2022,hrusto2026}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prometheus} --- metrics: CPU, memory, network, request latency, error rates and custom application counters.
  \item \textbf{Loki} --- structured application logs, container logs and Kubernetes event logs.
  \item \textbf{Tempo} --- OpenTelemetry-instrumented distributed traces of service-to-service calls.
  \item \textbf{Kubernetes events} --- OOMKilled and CrashLoopBackOff signals, the ``E'' of MELT.
  \item \textbf{Apache Kafka} \cite{kreps2011kafka} --- an event backbone that carries per-window telemetry from TraceFlix to the detectors as a stream; it is the deployment realisation of the online pipeline, and the substrate the local-LLM detector also consumes (\S\ref{sec:method-streaming}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The service mesh (Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh}).} The application is a nine-service mesh with a genuine, depth-four call graph and real per-service business logic, not a uniform stub. A \texttt{gateway} entrypoint aggregates a home page by fanning out to \texttt{movie} (which calls \texttt{actor} and \texttt{review}), \texttt{user} (which enriches a profile with \texttt{auth} and \texttt{recommendation}) and \texttt{search}; \texttt{recommendation} and \texttt{search} both read the shared \texttt{catalog} leaf. This depth is what makes localisation non-trivial: because a fault at a leaf propagates a secondary latency symptom up \emph{every} ancestor on its path, several services are implicated at once, and only the originating-error-span feature disambiguates them (\S\ref{sec:res-rq2})---a structure a flat three-service chain cannot exhibit.

\paragraph{The Kafka event backbone (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataflow}).} Telemetry is transported as an event stream on Apache Kafka \cite{kreps2011kafka}. A producer publishes one record per window to the \texttt{tf.telemetry.windows} topic; the online ML detector and the LLM detector consume it \emph{independently}, each in its own consumer group, and publish a binary verdict to \texttt{tf.anomalies} tagged by detector, so the two are directly comparable on the same windows. Decoupling collection from detection from reasoning lets each stage scale and fail independently, and makes the per-window prequential protocol (test-then-train) a property of the transport rather than of a single process. For reproducibility the RQ3 results of \S\ref{sec:res-rq3} are computed on the \emph{deterministic in-process} prequential harness; the Kafka backbone is the deployment realisation of the identical per-window protocol, and any broker-induced latency or loss it introduces is a deployment concern (\S\ref{sec:disc-threats}), not a property of the reported numbers.

\paragraph{The local-LLM detector (Fig.~\ref{fig:llm}).} The second consumer is a local large language model, Qwen2.5-3B \cite{qwen2025}, served on-host by Ollama \cite{ollama2024}. Rather than the engineered feature vector, it receives each window's \emph{raw} MELT signals, formatted into a compact prompt comprising a rule sheet of the six fault signatures and a few in-context exemplars sampled from the training split; it returns a strict JSON binary verdict $\{\textsf{anomaly},\textsf{confidence}\}$ that is parsed into the same $0/1$ output as every other detector. An optional LoRA adapter \cite{hu2022lora} specialises the base model to this mesh's signatures; the lift of tuning over the base prompt is the quantity of interest. Its intended role is complementary rather than competitive: \S\ref{sec:res-rq3} shows the per-window streaming detector must be \emph{cheap and bounded}---properties a 3B-parameter model does not have---so the LLM is best positioned as an on-demand reasoning and triage layer over already-flagged windows, not as the primary per-window detector.

\subsection{Fault injection and ground-truth labelling}\label{sec:method-faults}

Ground-truth labels are generated by controlled fault injection following the timestamped-injection methodology of Pedroso et al.\ \cite{pedroso2025}. Faults are applied to running pods with precise start/stop timestamps, which become the labels against which detector output is scored. The fault taxonomy spans resource-exhaustion, performance-degradation and availability modes: memory leaks, CPU saturation, latency spikes, error bursts, OOMKilled events, CrashLoopBackOff, connection-pool exhaustion and network partitions. To produce genuine Kubernetes events for the ``E'' signal used by C4, container resource limits are patched so that memory-leak faults escalate to real OOMKills rather than simulated ones. A load generator drives continuous, realistic traffic so that each fault rides on a live request stream rather than an idle system; each \emph{episode} selects a service as the fault origin, applies the fault, and lets dependent services exhibit secondary effects (e.g.\ induced latency), reproducing realistic fault propagation for RCA.

\subsection{Telemetry configurations (the completeness axis)}\label{sec:method-configs}

The completeness axis is operationalised as four ordered, nested configurations (Table~\ref{tab:configs}). Feature engineering is \emph{configuration-aware}: only the feature families permitted by the active configuration are assembled, so an identical telemetry window yields different feature vectors per configuration. This is the mechanism that isolates signal availability as the independent variable.

Feature engineering operates on the telemetry falling within each fixed-length window and aggregates it into a flat vector, so that all model families consume a common tabular representation. The feature families added by each pillar are as follows (the full per-feature listing is given in Appendix~\ref{app:features}). The metrics pillar (C1) contributes per-window aggregates of resource and request signals---CPU and memory utilisation, request and error rates, and latency percentiles---which are the signals available to any team with basic monitoring. Logs (C2) add volume- and severity-based features (error- and warning-log counts and a log-template diversity measure) that disambiguate anomalies whose metric signature is equivocal. Traces (C3) add span-level features---span rate, mean span duration, error-span rate---and, critically for localisation, the \emph{originating-error-span} feature that identifies the service at which an error tree begins rather than the services that merely propagate its latency. Events and history (C4) add Kubernetes lifecycle-event counts (OOMKilled, CrashLoopBackOff, restarts) and rolling-baseline deviation features that encode how far the current window departs from a longer historical norm.

Two properties of this design matter for the validity of the completeness comparison. First, the feature families are \emph{additive and disjoint}: enabling a pillar adds its features without altering those of lower pillars, so an identical telemetry window produces feature vectors that are strict prefixes of one another as $c$ increases. Second, all features are window-local aggregates, deliberately avoiding high-cardinality raw identifiers (\S\ref{sec:bg-tooling}); this keeps the representation production-realistic and ensures the online model's per-window update cost is bounded by a small, fixed dimensionality rather than growing with traffic.

\subsection{Drift regimes (the temporal axis)}\label{sec:method-regimes}

Non-stationarity is induced as a controlled sequence of operating regimes, each representing a common operational event that shifts the telemetry distribution:
\begin{description}
  \item[$R_0$ --- Baseline.] The nominal operating point; the offline models train here.
  \item[$R_1$ --- Latency regression.] A deployment-style shift that raises baseline service latency, moving the ``normal'' latency distribution.
  \item[$R_2$ --- Scale-out.] An autoscaling event that changes replica counts and therefore per-pod resource-utilisation baselines.
  \item[$R_3$ --- Combined load.] A traffic surge superimposed on the previous shifts---the hardest, most-drifted regime, moving multiple distributions at once.
\end{description}
The stream is processed prequentially over $320$ episodes and $34{,}560$ windows (nine services per episode-window), of which $8{,}640$ constitute the $R_0$ warm-up and $25{,}920$ the drifted future ($R_1$--$R_3$) on which RQ3 is scored.

\subsection{Learners and the online model}\label{sec:method-learners}

Four learners instantiate the temporal axis: \texttt{offline\_static} (a Random Forest trained once on $R_0$ then frozen), \texttt{offline\_periodic} (the same model refit every $500$ windows on a $2{,}880$-window buffer), \texttt{online\_adaptive} (a streaming SGD model with adaptive normalisation and a champion bandit, updated by prequential \texttt{partial\_fit} one window at a time), and an \texttt{offline\_full} oracle trained on \emph{all} regimes as a reference ceiling. The offline learners use a Random Forest, chosen because the model-family comparison (RQ4, \S\ref{sec:res-rq4}, which we report last) identifies ensemble trees as the strongest family on this multimodal tabular telemetry.

The online detector combines three mechanisms designed to track drift without retaining history (Algorithm~\ref{alg:online}). First, an incrementally-fitted linear classifier (stochastic gradient descent, logistic loss) updates on every window. Second, an exponentially-weighted (EW) running normaliser rescales each feature using running estimates of its mean and variance, so that a shift in a feature's baseline---the signature of drift---is absorbed by the normaliser rather than corrupting the decision boundary. Third, a \emph{champion bandit} maintains a small pool of candidate learning-rate/regularisation configurations and re-selects the current champion by recent prequential F1 whenever a candidate overtakes the incumbent, providing drift-triggered adaptation of the update dynamics themselves.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Online-adaptive prequential detection}\label{alg:online}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} telemetry stream; candidate set $\mathcal{H}=\{(\eta_0,\alpha)\}$
\State Initialise EW normaliser $\mathcal{N}$; models $\{m_h\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}$; champion $h^\star \gets$ default
\For{each window $t = 1, 2, \dots$}
  \State $\mathbf{x}_t \gets \phi_c(\text{telemetry}_t)$ \Comment{configuration-aware features}
  \State $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t \gets \mathcal{N}.\textsc{transform}(\mathbf{x}_t)$ \Comment{rescale by running mean/var}
  \State $\hat{y}_t \gets m_{h^\star}.\textsc{predict}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t)$ \Comment{test before train}
  \State \textbf{emit} $\hat{y}_t$
  \State observe label $y_t$
  \State $\mathcal{N}.\textsc{update}(\mathbf{x}_t)$
  \For{$h \in \mathcal{H}$} \Comment{update all candidates}
     \State $m_h.\textsc{partial\_fit}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t, y_t)$
     \State update rolling prequential F1 of $m_h$
  \EndFor
  \If{$\arg\max_h \text{F1}(m_h) \neq h^\star$} \Comment{drift-triggered re-selection}
     \State $h^\star \gets \arg\max_h \text{F1}(m_h)$; record adapt event
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The three mechanisms have the following precise form. The classifier maintains a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ updated by stochastic gradient descent on the logistic loss with $\ell_2$ strength $\alpha$ and an inverse-scaling learning rate from $\eta_0$; the per-window update touches only the $d_c$ active weights and is therefore $O(d_c)$ in time and memory, with no dependence on stream length. The exponentially-weighted normaliser maintains, for each feature $j$, running estimates updated every window with decay $\lambda$,
\begin{align}
  \mu_{t,j} &= (1-\lambda)\,\mu_{t-1,j} + \lambda\, x_{t,j}, \\
  s^2_{t,j} &= (1-\lambda)\,s^2_{t-1,j} + \lambda\,(x_{t,j}-\mu_{t,j})^2,
\end{align}
and rescales the input as $\tilde{x}_{t,j} = (x_{t,j}-\mu_{t,j})/\sqrt{s^2_{t,j}+\varepsilon}$. Because $\mu$ and $s^2$ track the moving feature distribution, a virtual-drift shift in a feature's baseline (\S\ref{sec:prob-drift}) is absorbed into the normaliser rather than presented to the classifier as a spurious anomaly---the mechanism that protects against the stale-normal precision collapse. The champion bandit maintains the candidate pool $\mathcal{H} = \{\,(\eta_0,\alpha) : \eta_0 \in \{0.01,0.05,0.1\},\ \alpha \in \{10^{-4},10^{-3}\}\,\}$, scores each by prequential F1 over a trailing window, and re-selects the champion $h^\star$ only when a challenger exceeds the incumbent by a margin $\delta$ (a hysteresis that prevents thrashing). Re-selection events are logged as ``adapt events'' (\S\ref{sec:res-rq3-adapt}); their frequency indicates how hard the drift is to track at a given telemetry richness.

For RQ4 the model-family comparison additionally evaluates, on the richest configuration C4, Random Forest, two gradient-boosting implementations, an LSTM temporal model, and an attention-style late-fusion model drawing on the building-blocks strategy of HolisticRCA \cite{han2024}. For RQ2 the localisation component ranks candidate fault entities, comparing a metrics-and-logs localiser (C2 features) against one that additionally consumes the trace-derived originating-error-span signal (C3 features), thereby isolating the marginal RCA value of tracing.

\subsection{Evaluation and cost metrics}\label{sec:method-metrics}

Detection is evaluated with precision, recall, F1 and AUC-ROC. F1 is the headline metric because anomaly data are class-imbalanced (normal windows dominate), under which raw accuracy is misleading---a trivial always-normal predictor scores high on accuracy but has zero recall. For the streaming experiments, F1 is computed \emph{prequentially} over the future ($R_1$--$R_3$) windows. Root-cause localisation is measured by top-$k$ accuracy; on the nine-service mesh top-$k$ does \emph{not} saturate without traces (multi-hop latency implicates several ancestors), so the trace-driven lift is visible across $k$ and \textbf{top-1 is the strictest measure}.

Because RQ3 reframes the choice of paradigm as an operational decision, we additionally report operational-cost metrics that a static accuracy comparison omits: \emph{maximum per-window latency} (the tail cost, which exposes retraining spikes), \emph{model footprint} in kilobytes, the number of \emph{retained historical windows} (a data-governance and memory cost), and \emph{total CPU time}. These let us characterise the accuracy/cost trade-off rather than accuracy alone.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:method-repro}

All models are trained and evaluated under a fixed random seed, an identical train/test protocol and the same preprocessing, so cross-cell differences reflect configuration and policy rather than stochastic training variation. The synthetic prequential stream is generated deterministically. All tooling is open-source, and the result datasets (\texttt{rq1\_completeness}, \texttt{rq2\_localisation}, \texttt{rq3\_online\_vs\_offline}, \texttt{rq3\_cost}, \texttt{rq3\_timeline}, \texttt{rq4\_model\_family} and their summaries) underpin every figure and table reported below.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We report the four questions in turn. RQ1 and RQ2 characterise what the telemetry makes possible---how completeness affects detection once the stream is non-stationary, and how distributed traces enable root-cause localisation. RQ3 is the article's headline: it compares the static, periodic and online learning paradigms on the drifting stream and isolates their interaction with completeness. Two conclusions follow. First, completeness matters under non-stationarity, not only on a clean held-out set: richer telemetry raises the attainable detection on a drifting stream, with distributed traces the single most valuable pillar---a more specific finding than the literature's general ``more is better'' \cite{lomio2022,han2024}, and one that the near-equivalence of C3 and C4 sharpens further, since for detection the three classical pillars already capture almost all available signal. Second, that this gain materialises at all presupposes a detector that keeps current: whether the telemetry or the \emph{learning paradigm} is the binding constraint under drift---and how the two interact---is precisely the subject of RQ3. \textbf{Completeness raises the attainable detection; whether adaptation realises it is the question we reach in RQ3.} We turn first to the localisation contribution of traces (RQ2).

\subsection{RQ2: What does distributed tracing add to localisation?}\label{sec:res-rq2}

Table~\ref{tab:rq2} reports root-cause localisation with traces excluded (C2) versus included (C3). The introduction of traces raises top-1 accuracy from $0.7692$ to a perfect $1.0000$, eliminating the localisation errors the metric-and-log approach commits.

The mechanism, as anticipated in the design, lies in the originating-error-span feature. Under the metric-and-log approach a downstream fault raises the latency of \emph{every} service on its call path---in the nine-service mesh a fault at a leaf such as \texttt{catalog-service} induces a secondary latency symptom in all of its transitive callers---so the anomaly score implicates as many as four or five ancestors almost as strongly as the true culprit, and a dependent is frequently ranked first. The depth of the graph is what makes this hard: top-$k$ no longer saturates without traces (top-2 $=0.897$, top-3 $=0.949$), because several plausible culprits must be disambiguated rather than just one of three. The trace feature resolves the ambiguity completely: only the originating service accumulates originating error spans, pulling the true root cause unambiguously to the top and attaining perfect localisation at top-1, and hence at every $k$. This is precisely the controlled, ablation-style evidence the literature was shown to lack: where Han et al.\ \cite{han2024} demonstrate that a fused multimodal architecture beats single-modal baselines, we isolate the trace modality and quantify its marginal contribution while holding all else constant.

\subsection{RQ3: Does the learning paradigm matter under drift?}\label{sec:res-rq3}

RQ1 and RQ2 measured a controlled, \emph{held-out baseline}---detection accuracy and root-cause localisation for a model trained once on a single distribution. We now re-use the identical pipeline and feature sets on the drifted future stream ($25{,}920$ post-warm-up windows across $R_1$--$R_3$) and compare the three realisable learning policies, with the all-regimes oracle as a ceiling.

\subsubsection{Headline: F1 on the drifted stream}\label{sec:res-rq3-head}

Table~\ref{tab:rq3-head} and Fig.~\ref{fig:rq3head} report F1 over the future stream. The result inverts the baseline picture. The frozen batch model collapses to F1 $\approx 0.36$ \emph{regardless} of how much telemetry it is given: going from metrics-only to full MELT moves its F1 by barely a point ($0.3602 \rightarrow 0.3705$), with its precision pinned near $0.22$ throughout. The two adaptive policies recover almost the entire gap, but \emph{which} of them leads depends on telemetry richness. Under thin telemetry (C1, C2) the online and periodic learners are within $0.007$ F1 of each other---periodic marginally ahead ($0.8204$ vs.\ $0.8130$ at C1)---because with few signals the online normaliser has little to re-centre on and much of the drift there is virtual (\S\ref{sec:res-rq3-regime}). Once distributed traces are present (C3, C4) the online-adaptive model pulls clearly ahead, reaching $0.9737$ under C3 and $0.9755$ under C4 and beating periodic by $\sim 0.05$. In every configuration the online model also exceeds the all-regimes oracle ($0.8124$/$0.8166$/$0.9294$/$0.9267$ for C1--C4; Table~\ref{tab:rq3-regime} note), because the oracle still fits a single static boundary across all regimes whereas the online model tracks each regime as it arrives.

Two features deserve emphasis. The more informative the features, the more a continuous learner extracts from each window relative to a lagging batch refit---so distributed traces are precisely what tip the paradigm choice decisively toward online, and under thin telemetry the cheaper periodic schedule remains competitive.

\subsubsection{Precision, recall and the stale-normal signature}\label{sec:res-rq3-prf}

The F1 headline conceals \emph{how} each policy fails, which Table~\ref{tab:rq3-prf} makes explicit by decomposing the future-stream score into precision, recall and AUC-ROC. The static model exhibits the stale-normal signature in its purest form: its recall is near-perfect ($0.98$--$1.00$) while its precision collapses to $\approx 0.22$ at every configuration. That combination is diagnostic---the frozen boundary still fires on the genuine anomalies (high recall) but, because the post-drift normal has moved into the region it labels anomalous, it also fires on healthy traffic, so the overwhelming majority of its alerts are false. No amount of additional telemetry corrects this: the precision is flat across C1--C4 because the failure is in the boundary's \emph{location}, not in the features' \emph{informativeness}. The online model shows the opposite profile, trading a little recall for a large precision gain ($0.87$--$0.99$), which is the operationally desirable direction given the cost of alert fatigue.

\subsubsection{Adaptation behaviour}\label{sec:res-rq3-adapt}

How often the online model re-selects its champion tracks how hard the drift is to follow. Richer telemetry requires \emph{fewer} corrective adapt events---seven champion switches at C1, three at C2, and \emph{none} once traces are added (C3/C4), whereas the periodic schedule refits a fixed $51$ times regardless. This is a second face of the completeness$\times$adaptation interaction: richer signal not only raises accuracy but stabilises adaptation, because more drift is absorbed by the EW normaliser and less must be chased by re-selecting the learner.

\subsection{RQ4: Which model family best exploits multimodal telemetry?}\label{sec:res-rq4}

We return, last, to a question deferred from the held-out baseline analysis: which model family this pipeline should use, and---now that RQ3 has shown a continuously-adapting detector to be mandatory under drift---what that choice implies for the online learner. The implication only becomes legible once the online setting is in view, which is why this question is answered after, rather than before, the drift comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:rq4} compares five model families on the richest configuration (C4) with identical inputs. Ensemble trees are the most effective family: the three tree models cluster within $0.004$ F1 of one another---gradient boosting highest at $0.988$, Random Forest at $0.986$, and the second boosting implementation at $0.984$---each exceeding $0.98$ precision, few false alarms, an operationally desirable property given the cost of alert fatigue. The temporal (LSTM) and late-fusion models underperform, instructively rather than disappointingly: the windowed feature representation already captures much per-window signal---and, being assembled from interleaved per-service windows, offers a sequential model little coherent temporal structure---so the LSTM trails badly ($0.259$ F1); the late-fusion architecture's requirement of cross-pillar agreement suppresses false positives at the cost of recall ($0.999$ precision against $0.804$ recall).

The lesson, on which the drift comparison of RQ3 has already drawn, is that the benefit of rich multimodal data is conditioned on the model's capacity to exploit it: a powerful, data-hungry architecture does not automatically beat a simpler ensemble when the feature representation is already informative. This is why the offline learners in RQ3 used a Random Forest, and why the online learner is a lightweight linear model---on tabular telemetry, simplicity buys both accuracy and the cheap, bounded per-window update that streaming requires.

This is the apparent tension the article's ordering was designed to resolve: if ensemble trees are the strongest \emph{batch} family, why is the \emph{online} learner---now shown to be the one that matters under drift---not also a tree? The answer is that the two settings reward different properties. A Random Forest is an excellent \emph{static} estimator but a poor \emph{incremental} one: it cannot be updated by a single example without either refitting (the expensive, bursty periodic path) or resorting to incremental-tree variants whose drift behaviour in the upper splits is fragile. The online setting prizes a bounded, smooth per-window update and graceful adaptation over raw stationary accuracy, and a normalised linear model delivers exactly that---its per-update cost is $O(d_c)$, its footprint is kilobytes, and, crucially, the configuration-aware features (\S\ref{sec:method-configs}) have already done the non-linear work of summarising raw telemetry into informative aggregates, leaving a decision surface that is close to linearly separable. The RQ3 results already reported vindicate the choice: once the telemetry is rich, the linear online model beats both batch paradigms on the drifted stream and, under full telemetry, exceeds the Random-Forest oracle itself (Table~\ref{tab:rq3-head}). The strongest \emph{static} model and the strongest \emph{streaming} model are simply not the same model, and conflating the two is one way the single-snapshot literature is misled.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}\label{sec:disc-threats}

The principal external-validity caveat is that the RQ3 stream, though drift-controlled and built on production-representative telemetry, is synthetic-prequential; absolute F1 values are testbed-specific. We stress that the contribution is the \emph{ordering and its reversal}, not the absolute numbers: that the baseline-best ranking does not survive drift, and that online dominates static at every cell and leads periodic in $8$ of $12$ regime$\times$configuration cells (the four exceptions being virtual-drift scale-out and combined-load under thin telemetry, where the cheaper periodic schedule is competitive), is robust to the absolute scale. The nine-service topology, though deeper than many controlled testbeds, is still a single synthetic mesh, which bounds topological generalisation; on it top-$k$ remains informative even without traces (Table~\ref{tab:rq2}). The online model depends on a usable label signal; where labels are scarce or heavily delayed, semi-supervised or unsupervised drift-adaptation would be required---a direction for future work. On internal validity, the held-constant controls (model, seed, scenarios, load) and the nested within-subjects design make cross-cell differences attributable to the manipulated factors, and fault-injection timing jitter is mitigated by recording exact start/stop timestamps. Finally, a single seed underlies the reported run; the effect sizes are large relative to plausible seed variance, but a multi-seed study would tighten the conclusion-validity argument.

The streaming and LLM additions (\S\ref{sec:method-streaming}) are reported conservatively for the same reason. The RQ3 numbers come from the deterministic in-process prequential harness, not a loaded Kafka broker, so broker-induced tail latency, consumer lag and loss-on-rebalance are out of scope; they would, if anything, only tighten the per-window latency budget the online model already meets, and a broker is additionally a shared dependency a deployment must size for. The served Qwen2.5-3B detector is implemented but not yet evaluated at scale---we therefore make no accuracy claim for it, and the clearly-marked heuristic fallback is a sanity scaffold, not a proxy for the model. Because that detector consumes \emph{raw}, attacker-influenceable log and trace text, it also carries an indirect prompt-injection exposure \cite{greshake2023} that the engineered-feature classifiers, which read only numeric aggregates, do not; treating telemetry text as untrusted before it reaches the prompt is future work.

It is worth being explicit about what a full real-deployment validation would add, and why we regard the present controlled study as the necessary first step rather than a substitute. On a live production stream, drift is neither scheduled nor labelled: regime changes arrive unannounced and entangled, ground truth is partial and delayed (often reconstructed after the fact from incident timelines), and the fault distribution is long-tailed in ways no injection taxonomy fully reproduces. A field study would therefore measure something our testbed cannot---robustness to \emph{unknown} drift and to \emph{imperfect} labels---but at the price of the very thing that makes our central claim interpretable: the ability to attribute the reversal between the held-out baseline and drifted rankings to drift alone, with the model, features, faults and seed held fixed. The two evidence types are complementary, and in the order presented: the controlled result establishes \emph{that} the learning paradigm dominates completeness under drift and \emph{why}; a subsequent field deployment would establish \emph{how much} of that advantage survives the additional noise of production. We read the present findings as a strong prior for that field study, not as a replacement for it, consistent with the synthetic-versus-operational tension that motivated the design \cite{hrusto2026,islam2021}.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:background}
%%===========================================================%%

Having presented our results, we now situate the study within the literature, at the intersection of four areas: observability in cloud-native systems (\S\ref{sec:bg-obs}), machine-learning anomaly detection (\S\ref{sec:bg-ad}), observability-driven root-cause analysis (\S\ref{sec:bg-rca}), and---the axis the present work foregrounds---concept drift and online learning over telemetry streams (\S\ref{sec:bg-drift}). Section~\ref{sec:bg-eval} considers the persistent divide between synthetic and operational evaluation, and \S\ref{sec:bg-gap} synthesises the gap.

\subsection{Observability in cloud-native systems}\label{sec:bg-obs}

Observability, borrowed from control theory (a system is observable if its internal state can be reconstructed from its outputs), is apt for a microservice mesh too large and ephemeral to inspect directly: its state must be \emph{inferred} from emitted telemetry \cite{kosinska2023}. In a systematic mapping of 56 primary studies, Kosi\'nska et al.\ \cite{kosinska2023} establish that cloud-native observability is operationalised through monitoring, logging and tracing, and identify a research imbalance in which monitoring dominates---an imbalance that foreshadows an \emph{evaluation} imbalance: if most detectors are built and tested on metrics alone, the marginal value of logs and traces is left uncharacterised \cite{faseeha2025observability}. The practitioner literature crystallises the same pillars into the MELT model and an end-to-end pipeline \cite{boten2022,chapman2023}, emphasising two themes that bear on this paper: observability is sold as the foundation for \emph{automated} analysis rather than dashboard-watching \cite{beginnersobs,aws2022obs}, and it is a \emph{cost} as much as a capability---instrumentation overhead, data-engineering load and high-cardinality storage are real expenses \cite{leadingobs,kosinska2023}. The economic premise of RQ1 follows: if each additional pillar carries a cost, practitioners need evidence on its detection benefit.

\subsection{The observability pipeline and its tooling}\label{sec:bg-tooling}

The modern observability pipeline, documented in depth by the practitioner literature \cite{boten2022,chapman2023,aws2022obs,beginnersobs}, is built around \emph{OpenTelemetry}, the de facto vendor-neutral instrumentation layer \cite{boten2022,opentelemetry2024spec}: it defines a common data model for the three pillars (metric, log record and span) and a \emph{collector} that exports to backends. Because an OpenTelemetry-instrumented application emits the same schema whether small or production-scale, building our testbed on it directly addresses the concern that synthetic data may not resemble operational data \cite{hrusto2026}. Boten \cite{boten2022} stresses that instrumentation carries the highest leverage and the highest cost, and that tracing is the most expensive pillar: every request generates a tree of spans, so production deployments apply head- or tail-based \emph{sampling} that trades completeness for cost---an active research line tries to soften this trade-off with smarter sampling \cite{huang2025mint,wu2025tracesampling} or trace recovery without instrumentation \cite{ashok2024traceweaver}, and the point that observability spend must be justified by return is now made explicitly \cite{hausenblas2023roi}. The marginal detection value of traces (RQ1, RQ2) therefore has real economic stakes rather than being a foregone conclusion.

On the storage and query side, Chapman and Holmes \cite{chapman2023} document the now-common ``LGTM'' stack---Loki, Grafana, Tempo and a Prometheus-compatible store---which is exactly the backend topology our testbed adopts (\S\ref{sec:method-testbed}), and stress two realities for machine-learning consumers: high-cardinality labels explode time-series count, so feature engineering must aggregate over those dimensions rather than consume them raw; and metrics, logs and traces are separately indexed, so joining them is a non-trivial workflow that a pillar-fusing detector performs automatically (\S\ref{sec:method-configs}). The industry guides \cite{aws2022obs,beginnersobs,leadingobs} further frame observability as a maturity curve---metrics, then logging, then tracing, then correlation and automated analysis---which is the completeness axis of RQ1 made organisational (C1$\rightarrow$C4), with each step adding cost whose return is not self-evident; replacing that ``more is better'' intuition with a measured marginal value per pillar is exactly what our completeness study provides.

\subsection{Anomaly-detection techniques}\label{sec:bg-ad}

Raeiszadeh et al.\ \cite{raeiszadeh2026} provide the most current taxonomy of ML-based anomaly detection for cloud-native architectures and, valuably, evaluate the field against six requirements: adaptability to dynamic topologies, distributed operation, interpretability, scalability with high-velocity telemetry, real-time detection latency, and cross-layer telemetry integration. Their central finding is that existing approaches systematically fail on adaptability, distributed operation and interpretability. We adopt their requirements lens as an evaluative yardstick, and note that \emph{adaptability}---the ability to track a moving distribution---is exactly the requirement a static evaluation cannot test and that RQ3 is designed to probe.

\paragraph{Classical machine learning.} Traditional algorithms---Support Vector Machines, Isolation Forest, Random Forest and gradient-boosted trees---remain competitive for metric-based detection. Lomio et al.\ \cite{lomio2022} train ML models on 168 runtime metrics from Kafka and Zookeeper, reporting AUC above 95\% for performance anomalies with training times of 8--17 minutes. The result is a useful baseline but illustrates the field's characteristic limitations: the evaluation is single-modality and batch, a model trained once on a static snapshot. Neither property survives contact with a streaming, multimodal, non-stationary environment---the point RQ3 makes empirical.

\paragraph{Deep and temporal models.} Deep learning attracts interest for its capacity to model temporal and spatial structure. Dkmak et al.\ \cite{dkmak2025} introduce the \emph{Night's Watch} algorithm, a variational-autoencoder architecture integrating multi-source data with temporal features, reporting precision up to 92\% in microservice anomaly detection. Recurrent architectures (LSTM, GRU) capture temporal dependencies in metric streams, and graph neural networks encode service-topology relations. Across this sub-field an \emph{accuracy--interpretability--cost} trilemma recurs: deep models report strong precision but are opaque (violating the interpretability requirement of \cite{raeiszadeh2026}), are expensive to train, and frequently require the very multimodal data whose marginal value has not been isolated. The literature thus offers increasingly sophisticated \emph{models} while leaving the prior question---how much \emph{signal} they actually need, and whether they can be \emph{kept current}---unanswered.

\paragraph{The recent surge, by modality.} Since then the field has fragmented along the three pillars. For metrics, deep models for multivariate time series now dominate, with surveys cataloguing a zoo of reconstruction-, forecasting-, attention- and contrastive-learning architectures \cite{wang2025mtssurvey,xia2024coupled,choi2024selfsup} and the question of which metrics carry signal studied in its own right \cite{singal2025metric}---even as critiques argue much reported progress is an artefact of flawed evaluation protocols \cite{sarfraz2024quovadis,wagner2025metrics}. For logs, the trajectory runs from template-sequence models to pre-trained transformers and large language models that read raw log text \cite{landauer2022logsurvey,guan2024logllm,pospieszny2025adalog}. For traces, graph deep learning encoding the call graph and span timings---often fused with metric channels---has become the default \cite{chen2023tracegra,akmeemana2025galmad,zhang2025graphneural,alomari2025context}. Each pillar thus has its own state of the art, yet the three are seldom measured against one another on the same system---precisely the comparison RQ1 makes.

\paragraph{Multimodal fusion and the LLM turn.} A newer strand fuses the pillars outright: contrastive and diffusion-based architectures learn a joint representation and report gains over any single modality \cite{wang2025multimodal,zhang2026modify}, and large multimodal models serve as few-shot anomaly analysers over heterogeneous signals \cite{zhuang2024multimodal}, within the broader \emph{AIOps} arc from bespoke models towards LLM-driven pipelines \cite{zhang2024aiops}. Two features matter here: this literature almost uniformly assumes more signal is better without isolating each pillar's marginal contribution, and it is overwhelmingly \emph{batch}, leaving behaviour once the distribution moves---the subject of RQ3---largely untouched. A few works lean the other way with forecasting- or online-flavoured detectors \cite{xu2024forecasting,wette2024omlad}, to which we return in \S\ref{sec:bg-drift}.

\paragraph{Observability-integrated, proactive detection.} Mart et al.\ \cite{mart2020} address anomaly detection in Kubernetes clusters using Prometheus metrics, observing that most monitoring remains \emph{reactive}, triggering only once defects are imminent, and proposing time-series forecasting for earlier detection. Their contribution is conceptual as much as technical: it reframes detection as a proactive, observability-integrated capability rather than a threshold alarm---a framing this paper inherits and extends from proactive to \emph{continually self-correcting}.

\subsection{Root-cause analysis through observability data}\label{sec:bg-rca}

Detection is rarely an end in itself; it serves root-cause analysis (RCA), whose effectiveness is tightly coupled to observability quality. Han et al.\ \cite{han2024} propose \emph{HolisticRCA}, formalising RCA along three dimensions---resource-entity localisation (\emph{which} service failed), observability-feature identification (\emph{which} signal indicates it), and fault-type classification (\emph{what kind} of failure)---and using a graph attention network to map heterogeneous trace, metric and log features into a shared space. Their most consequential finding for us is that single-modal RCA is internally inconsistent: a metric-based localiser may implicate one entity while a log-based classifier indicates a different fault type, and jointly modelling all modalities resolves the inconsistency. This is the strongest existing evidence that modalities are \emph{complementary} rather than redundant---but it is demonstrated through a single fused architecture, not a controlled ablation, so the marginal contribution of traces specifically remains entangled with model design. RQ2 isolates it.

Complementary approaches reinforce the value of dependency structure. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2018} build Bayesian networks from performance metrics (CloudRanger), showing that service-dependency graphs derived from observability data improve fault localisation. Chen et al.\ \cite{chen2025} extend multi-source detection to end-to-end service-function chains (cSFCAD), integrating data-plane and control-plane signals across virtualised network functions. Pedroso et al.\ \cite{pedroso2025} combine LLM-based log analysis with Bayesian networks for probabilistic root-cause inference, demonstrating that language models can extract structure from heterogeneous event data, and---relevant to our fault-injection design---ground their evaluation in controlled fault injection with recorded timestamps. Together these works establish that topology and multi-source fusion improve RCA, yet none varies observability completeness as a controlled factor, and none addresses the temporal validity of the learned model.

The RCA literature has since accelerated sharply \cite{wang2024rcasurvey}, its centre of gravity moving towards causal inference and online change-point reasoning that constructs a causal graph over services and metrics and traces the fault to its source \cite{pham2024rca,yao2024chainofevent,pham2024baro}---though a benchmark of twenty-one such methods found the ranking highly method- and dataset-dependent \cite{pham2024rca}---while large language models have entered RCA as cloud-incident diagnosers \cite{chen2024rca} and reinforcement-tuned failure localisers \cite{zhang2025thinkfl}, prompting dedicated benchmarks on standardised multimodal telemetry \cite{pham2025rcaeval}. None of it, though, unsettles the two observations above: completeness is not the controlled variable, and the learned model is assumed to remain valid as the system evolves.

\subsection{Concept drift and online learning}\label{sec:bg-drift}

The variable this paper foregrounds is largely absent from the cloud-native anomaly-detection literature but central to the streaming-learning literature and its substantial body of surveys \cite{arora2024drift,hovakimyan2024drift,lukats2025drift}. In an observability stream, \emph{concept drift}---a change in $P(\mathbf{x}, y)$ (\S\ref{sec:prob-drift})---is the norm, not the exception: deployments, autoscaling and traffic surges each move feature distributions, so a detector trained on yesterday's normal assigns high anomaly scores to today's healthy traffic, the failure mode we term \emph{stale-normal}.

Three families of response exist. A \emph{static} model is trained once and frozen; it is cheap and predictable but, by construction, cannot track drift. A \emph{periodically-retrained} model is refitted at intervals on a sliding window of recent labelled data; it tracks drift in discrete steps but lags by up to one retraining interval, must retain a labelled buffer, and incurs a bursty refit cost. An \emph{online} (incremental) model updates its parameters on every observation via partial fitting; it tracks drift continuously, retains no raw history, and spreads its cost smoothly, at the price of higher aggregate update work and a dependence on a usable label signal. The industrial-scale experience of Islam et al.\ \cite{islam2021} and the adaptability deficit identified by Raeiszadeh et al.\ \cite{raeiszadeh2026} both point to continual learning as the missing ingredient, but neither the surveys nor the primary studies reviewed above evaluate the three paradigms head-to-head on the same observability stream. That head-to-head comparison is RQ3.

The broader stream-learning literature offers a spectrum of online algorithms, from incrementally-updated linear models---the cheap, $O(d)$ stochastic-gradient classifiers our detector uses---through incremental decision trees (Hoeffding trees and variants, which capture non-linear structure but are more sensitive to drift in their upper splits) to explicit drift-detection wrappers and streaming ensembles that retire and spawn base learners as concepts change. A growing line of work applies these ideas to anomaly detection---incremental autoencoders that re-fit as the stream evolves \cite{li2023autoencoder}, dual drift-detection schemes \cite{li2024incremental}, and online learners shown to beat their batch counterparts once drift is present \cite{wette2024omlad}---while the MLOps community frames the same problem as \emph{model decay} \cite{leest2025mlmonitoring}. Two design lessons inform our system: \emph{normalisation under drift matters as much as the learner} (so our detector pairs the classifier with a running normaliser), and \emph{adaptation can be implicit} (our champion bandit is a lightweight form of the ensemble idea, re-selecting among a small pool by recent performance rather than maintaining a costly ensemble). The contribution of RQ3 is not a new online algorithm but a controlled measurement of what \emph{any} reasonable online learner buys, and at what cost, relative to the batch paradigms that dominate the cloud-native literature.

\subsection{Monitoring data and real-world evaluation}\label{sec:bg-eval}

Hrusto et al.\ \cite{hrusto2026} provide the field's most recent systematic mapping (104 papers) of anomaly detection evaluated with \emph{real-world} monitoring data, arguing that synthetic datasets do not adequately represent operational cloud environments and that results on them may not transfer. Islam et al.\ \cite{islam2021} corroborate this at IBM Cloud scale, finding threshold-based monitoring inadequate for the heterogeneity of real environments---a conclusion their 2025 follow-up reinforces while releasing a public industrial dataset \cite{islam2025cloud}. The community has answered the transferability problem on two fronts: a wave of openly released, labelled-fault microservice datasets and benchmarks across modalities \cite{bakhtin2025lo2,pham2025rcaeval,ping2026anomod}, frequently generated through chaos-engineering fault injection now mature enough for its own systematic review \cite{owotogbe2026chaos}; and a sharper scrutiny of evaluation itself, showing that popular metrics can reward near-random predictions and that headline gains often evaporate under stricter protocols \cite{sarfraz2024quovadis,wagner2025metrics}---a caution we take seriously in \S\ref{sec:method-metrics}.

This exposes a methodological double bind. Realistic non-stationarity argues for production data, yet \emph{controlled} manipulation of observability completeness \emph{and} drift is only cleanly achievable when the data-generating process is itself controlled---i.e.\ in a testbed with deliberate fault injection and deliberate regime changes. We resolve the bind, as \S\ref{sec:method} details, by using a production-representative testbed (real OpenTelemetry instrumentation and real Prometheus/Loki/Tempo backends) under controlled fault injection \emph{and} controlled drift, rather than either a purely synthetic benchmark or uncontrolled production logs.

\subsection{Synthesis and research gap}\label{sec:bg-gap}

Four convergent gaps emerge.

\begin{description}
  \item[Gap 1 --- Completeness is rarely the controlled variable.] Most techniques are evaluated on a single modality \cite{lomio2022,mart2020}, while the strongest multimodal evidence \cite{han2024} varies the \emph{model} rather than the \emph{signal set}. Few studies hold the model fixed and systematically vary completeness to measure the marginal value of each pillar.
  \item[Gap 2 --- The learning paradigm is almost never a variable.] Evaluation is overwhelmingly single-snapshot batch \cite{lomio2022,dkmak2025,han2024}. The static, periodic and online paradigms are not compared head-to-head on a common observability stream, despite adaptability being a named, unmet requirement \cite{raeiszadeh2026}.
  \item[Gap 3 --- The synthetic-versus-operational divide.] Evaluation is typically either synthetic-and-controlled or operational-and-uncontrolled, rarely both \cite{hrusto2026,islam2021}.
  \item[Gap 4 --- Minimum-viable observability and when to adapt.] There is little practical guidance on either question. Practitioners face a real instrumentation cost \cite{leadingobs,kosinska2023} and a real drift problem \cite{islam2021} with scant evidence to guide either decision.
\end{description}

Positioned against the most directly comparable studies along five axes---whether telemetry completeness, the learning paradigm and drift are each varied, whether root-cause localisation is addressed, and whether the telemetry is production-representative---the present work stands apart. Single-modality batch studies \cite{lomio2022,mart2020} vary neither the signal set nor the paradigm cleanly; the strongest multimodal and RCA works \cite{dkmak2025,han2024,wang2018,chen2025,pedroso2025} vary the \emph{model} rather than the controlled signal set and do not address drift; and the industrial drift evidence \cite{islam2021} does not vary completeness. No prior study varies \emph{both} the signal set and the learning paradigm, and none evaluates the static, periodic and online paradigms head-to-head under controlled drift on production-representative telemetry---which this work is the first to do.

This paper addresses Gap 2 as its primary target (RQ3) and Gaps 1, 3 and 4 as the controlled foundation that makes the RQ3 result interpretable. The design fixes the ML pipeline and varies, orthogonally, the signal set (RQ1--RQ2) and the learning paradigm under drift (RQ3) on a production-representative, fault-injected, drift-controlled testbed.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}
%%===========================================================%%

This paper asked whether, for a non-stationary observability stream, the learning paradigm matters more than telemetry richness, and how the two interact. By crossing four nested telemetry configurations with three learning policies on a single production-representative, fault-injected, drift-controlled testbed, we reached a two-part answer. On the held-out baseline, completeness is decisive: detection F1 rises monotonically from $0.896$ to $0.986$, traces are the single most valuable increment, ensemble trees are the strongest family, and traces lift root-cause top-1 localisation from $0.77$ to a perfect $1.0$ on a deep nine-service mesh. But under drift this ceiling does not survive a frozen model: the static detector collapses to F1 $\approx 0.36$ regardless of telemetry, periodic retraining recovers much of the gap but expensively, and a lightweight online-adaptive detector dominates once telemetry is rich---decisively at C3/C4 and across two-thirds of regimes, reaching $\approx 0.98$ under full MELT and beating an all-regimes oracle at every configuration---while being $9$--$20\times$ better on tail latency, $\sim 130$--$390\times$ smaller, and free of any data-retention requirement.

The synthesis is that telemetry completeness and continual learning are complementary, not substitutable: richer signals raise the attainable ceiling, but only adaptation realises it on a live, drifting system. The field's guiding question, ``does observability matter?'', is therefore incomplete---observability matters for what is \emph{achievable}, but keeping the model current is what makes the achievable \emph{actual}. Practitioners should invest in observability (at least to metrics${+}$logs${+}$traces) and continual learning \emph{together}, preferring bounded-latency online adaptation to bursty periodic retraining wherever a label signal and a latency budget coexist.

Future work follows several directions. \emph{Label efficiency}: extend the online comparison to semi- and self-supervised drift adaptation where labels are scarce or delayed. \emph{Topological generalisation}: extend the testbed to production-scale call graphs, testing whether trace-driven localisation remains complete as dependency structures grow more irregular. \emph{Drift diversity}: broaden the regime set to schema changes, dependency re-wiring and seasonal patterns, paired with explicit drift-detection signals that distinguish virtual from real drift at runtime---triggering heavier adaptation only on genuine concept change and conserving the steady-state CPU that is the online model's sole cost disadvantage. \emph{Operational integration}: close the loop from online detection through trace-driven localisation to automated remediation \cite{pedroso2025,han2024}. \emph{Evaluating the streaming and LLM additions} (\S\ref{sec:method-streaming}): quantify the served Qwen2.5-3B detector, base versus LoRA-tuned \cite{hu2022lora}, against the linear online model, measure the Kafka backbone under load, and harden the LLM detector against the telemetry-borne prompt-injection exposure noted in \S\ref{sec:disc-threats} \cite{greshake2023}. Finally, \emph{multi-seed} runs would convert the single-seed effect sizes into formal significance intervals and confirm the paradigm ordering is invariant to the synthetic stream.

Taken together, these directions share a single thesis, which is the contribution we most wish to carry forward: in a non-stationary world, a detector is never finished being trained. The observability community has invested heavily in \emph{seeing} more of the system; the evidence here is that comparable investment in \emph{relearning} what is seen is what converts that visibility into reliable detection.
